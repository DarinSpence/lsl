## 1. Executive Summary

LL is a practical business-launch system for **Logan Lawns (DBA LL; website/domain brand Logan Lawns)** for an 18-year-old high-school graduate and a business partner starting a neighborhood lawn-care company with very little startup capital.

The immediate objective is **not** to build sophisticated software. The objective is to help the owners:

1. Acquire their first 10 paying customers.
2. Make booking and payment extremely easy.
3. Understand how a real business makes money.
4. Separate revenue, expenses, profit, cash reserves, owner equity, reimbursements, and distributions.
5. Establish a fair written partnership arrangement before money creates disagreements.
6. Build recurring customers and use member-only add-on services to increase revenue.
7. Track customers, jobs, goals, and financial performance with simple tools.
8. Operate almost entirely from a phone.
9. Keep initial spending within an approximately **$300 startup budget**.
10. Add technology only when the business has earned the right to need it.

The system should be educational as well as operational. It should teach the owners **why** each process exists rather than simply automating everything for them.

The guiding principle is:

> **Get customers first. Keep the system simple. Learn the numbers. Automate only after the process works.**

# 2. Product Vision

Create a **mobile-first pocket business coach and operating system** that tells a first-time service-business owner:

- What should I do next?
- Who are my customers?
- What jobs do I have?
- How much should I charge?
- How much money did we actually make?
- What expenses must be paid before we pay ourselves?
- How much cash needs to stay in the business?
- How much can each partner safely distribute?
- Are we on track against our goals?
- What should we focus on this week?

The product should feel more like a simple game/dashboard/checklist than accounting software.

# 4. Business Model

## Core Services

The primary services are:

- Lawn mowing
- String trimming / weed eating

These should be intentionally simple and easy to price.

## Recurring Customer / Membership Concept

LL should encourage repeat service without requiring a traditional monthly subscription.

### LL Member Rule

A customer becomes an **LL Member** when they keep their **next two lawn-service appointments booked**.

Membership does **not** initially require:

- A monthly membership fee
- Prepayment for an entire season
- An open-ended contract

Instead, the customer earns Member status by creating future scheduling certainty for LL.

### Standard / One-Time Customer

Can purchase:

- Lawn mowing
- String trimming / weed eating
- Other standard services LL chooses to make publicly available

### LL Member

A customer with at least **two future lawn-service appointments booked** receives:

- Preferred Member pricing / discount on core lawn service
- Access to exclusive Member-only add-on services
- Easier repeat booking
- Greater scheduling priority where practical

Potential Member-only add-ons include:

- Pulling weeds
- Light yard cleanup
- Driveway / sidewalk washing
- Minor outdoor maintenance tasks that are safe, legal, and within LL's competence
- Tightening loose deck screws
- Other simple property-maintenance tasks

**Important:** Position the offer as a membership/convenience benefit, not merely as a discount.

Suggested value proposition:

> "Keep your next two lawn visits booked and become an LL Member. Members receive preferred pricing and access to exclusive add-on services."

This structure teaches an important business principle: LL is exchanging a modest pricing benefit for **future demand certainty, better route planning, lower selling effort, and more opportunities for add-on revenue**.

The membership rule should be easy to understand and easy to verify in the CRM.

# 7. Financial Education Model

One of the most important parts of this project is teaching the owners how money flows through a business.

The system must clearly distinguish:

## Revenue

Money charged to customers for services.

## Direct Costs / Cost of Service

Costs that rise as jobs are performed, such as:

- Fuel used for jobs
- Trimmer line
- Mower consumables
- Blade sharpening attributable to operations
- Job-specific supplies
- Payment processing fees where appropriate

## Gross Profit

**Revenue - Direct Costs = Gross Profit**

## Operating Expenses / Overhead

Examples:

- Advertising
- Software
- General insurance
- Website/domain
- Bank fees
- General equipment maintenance
- Administrative expenses

## Net Profit

**Gross Profit - Operating Expenses = Net Profit**

## Cash

Net profit is **not automatically the same thing as distributable cash**.

Before owner distributions, the company should:

1. Pay current bills.
2. Refill fuel.
3. Replace consumables.
4. Address necessary equipment maintenance.
5. Fund near-term expected expenses.
6. Maintain an agreed operating cash reserve.

Only then should remaining cash be considered for owner distributions.

---

# 8. Owner Equity and Distributions

The system should teach:

### Owner Contributions

Money owners put into the company increases owner equity.

Example:

- Partner A contributes $150.
- Partner B contributes $150.
- Total initial company cash = $300.
- Total owner contributions/equity = $300.

These contributions are **not business revenue**.

### Distributions

Owners may later remove available business cash as distributions, subject to:

- Available cash
- Required reserves
- Partnership agreement
- Profitability
- Applicable accounting/tax rules

The educational model should reinforce:

> "Money in the bank does not automatically mean money available to spend personally."

# 9. Partnership Economics

The owners must establish a simple written agreement before operating.

This is a business-planning template, **not legal advice**. A qualified local professional should review legal/tax questions as the business grows.

The agreement should address:

- Ownership percentages.
- Initial cash contributions.
- Equipment contributed by each owner.
- Who owns equipment personally versus through the business.
- Truck usage.
- Truck reimbursement.
- Profit/distribution percentages.
- Responsibilities.
- Who may approve purchases.
- How disagreements are handled.
- What happens if someone stops working.
- What happens if a partner leaves.
- How business assets are handled if the company closes.

# 10. Truck Reimbursement

If one partner provides the truck, do **not** casually change the profit split on every job.

Prefer a consistent policy.

Potential model:

1. The truck remains personally owned.
2. The company pays an agreed truck-use reimbursement.
3. Reimbursement is treated as a business operating cost before calculating distributable profit.
4. Remaining distributable profit follows the agreed ownership/distribution split.

The partners must explicitly agree on:

- Reimbursement method.
- Reimbursement rate.
- What fuel is included.
- Whether maintenance/wear is included.
- Who pays insurance.
- Whether reimbursement is per mile, per workday, per month, or another documented method.

The spreadsheet should allow scenarios such as:

- 50/50 distribution
- 55/45 distribution
- 50/50 after truck reimbursement
- Other agreed percentages

Do not hard-code 55/45 as the required structure.

# 17. Booking

Booking must feel more like a **barber/salon service scheduler** than a generic meeting calendar.

Customers should be able to:

1. Choose a service.
2. Enter their address/contact information.
3. See or request an available time.
4. Book from a phone.
5. Receive confirmation/reminders.
6. Book their next two appointments to unlock LL Member status.

## Preferred MVP Candidate - Setmore Free

Setmore Free is the preferred initial booking/front-desk candidate because it is designed around services and appointments rather than meetings.

At implementation time, verify current pricing and free-tier limits before setup.

Desired features:

- Service-based booking
- Multiple team members if useful
- Mobile-friendly booking page
- Appointment confirmations/reminders
- Links from Google, website, QR codes, and Facebook
- Easy future-appointment booking
- Simple staff calendar management

## Likely Upgrade Path - Square Appointments

Square Appointments should be evaluated later as the likely integrated scheduling + payments platform when LL's volume justifies moving away from separate tools.

The important migration question is not "What week are we on?" but:

> "Has the cost and friction of separate scheduling and payments become greater than the cost of an integrated system?"

## Booking Flow

Initial flow:

**QR / Google / Facebook → LL landing page or booking page → Choose/request service → Enter address → Select/request time → Confirmation**

Because lawn care is location-dependent, LL may initially use **Request Service / Request a Quote** instead of guaranteeing an appointment before yard size, address, and route feasibility are known.

## Member Booking Flow

After the first successful service, invite the customer to:

**Book the next two lawn visits → Become an LL Member → Receive preferred pricing + unlock Member-only add-ons**

The CRM should automatically or manually track whether two future appointments are on the books.

# 18. Payments

The payment strategy should intentionally evolve with the business.

## Phase 1 - Launch With Venmo Business

Use **Venmo Business** initially if its current terms, fees, and business-use requirements fit LL.

Why:

- Familiar to many customers
- Low setup complexity
- Easy to use from a phone
- Appropriate for very small transaction volume
- Avoids adding software before LL has customers

Also evaluate Stripe/Wave payment links if a customer needs another electronic option.

Do not use a personal Venmo account for business activity in a way that violates provider terms.

## Phase 2 - Monitor Friction

As jobs increase, track:

- Number of electronic payments
- Time spent matching payments to jobs
- Customers asking for cards/tap-to-pay
- Invoices requiring manual follow-up
- Payment reconciliation errors
- Need for deposits or integrated checkout
- Scheduling/payment duplication
- Monthly processing fees
- Number of payment tools customers are using

## Phase 3 - Evaluate Venmo Business → Square

The roadmap should include a formal **"Evaluate Venmo Business → Square"** milestone around **Weeks 9-12**, or earlier if LL grows faster.

Move earlier if one or more of these triggers becomes meaningful:

1. **Integrated scheduling + payments would eliminate duplicate work.**
2. **Customers increasingly want card or tap-to-pay options.**
3. **Matching Venmo payments to jobs becomes a weekly administrative burden.**
4. **LL is using multiple payment methods and reconciliation becomes confusing.**
5. **Square Appointments would replace both the separate scheduler and payment workflow.**
6. **Transaction volume makes a more integrated checkout/reconciliation system worthwhile.**
7. **The economics of Square's current fees/features are better for LL's actual transaction mix.**
8. **Recurring Member workflows are easier to administer inside Square.**

Do **not** migrate merely because the calendar reaches Week 9.

The decision should be based on actual operational pain and economics.

## Phase 4 - Square as the Integrated Front Desk / Cash Register

If the triggers are met, Square may become the primary system for:

- Scheduling
- Card payments
- Tap to Pay
- Payment links
- Customer checkout
- Job/payment reconciliation
- Potentially invoices and related payment workflows

Venmo Business may still remain available as a secondary customer payment option if useful and easy to reconcile.

## Payment Design Principle

Customers should be able to pay with minimal friction, but LL should use the **fewest payment systems necessary**.

Normal processing fees should be considered when setting prices rather than surprising customers with confusing last-minute charges.

# 37. Distribution Guardrails

Before distributing money:

- [ ] All completed jobs are recorded.
- [ ] All payments are reconciled.
- [ ] Fuel is funded.
- [ ] Consumables are replenished.
- [ ] Required maintenance is funded.
- [ ] Known bills are funded.
- [ ] Truck reimbursement is recorded.
- [ ] Cash reserve is at or above target.
- [ ] No significant upcoming expense has been ignored.
- [ ] Distribution calculation follows the written agreement.

If any item fails, reduce or postpone distributions.

---

# 38. Partnership Guardrails

The owners should agree that:

- Business money is not personal money.
- Expenses require receipts or documentation.
- Reimbursements follow an agreed policy.
- No one changes the distribution split casually.
- Major purchases require both partners' approval.
- Customer money goes through the business process.
- All jobs must be recorded.
- Both owners can see the business numbers.
- Disagreements are discussed before money is withdrawn.

---

# 39. Customer Experience Guardrails

The business should strive to:

- Respond quickly.
- Show up when promised.
- Communicate delays.
- Leave the property clean.
- Close gates.
- Avoid damaging property.
- Be polite.
- Make payment easy.
- Make recurring service easy.
- Ask satisfied customers for reviews.

Reliability may be a stronger differentiator than low price.

# 40. Safety and Scope Guardrails

The system should not encourage the owners to accept work outside their competence.

Before offering add-ons, consider:

- Safety
- Required licensing
- Insurance
- Equipment
- Property-damage risk
- Local laws
- Whether the task is appropriate for the business

"Minor handyman work" must not become unqualified electrical, structural, roofing, plumbing, pesticide, tree-removal, or other regulated/high-risk work.

# 53. Mentor Teaching Sequence

The mentor should teach concepts in this order:

### Lesson 1 - A business is not revenue

"You can collect $1,000 and still make very little money."

### Lesson 2 - Costs come before distributions

"Replace what the business consumed before paying yourself."

### Lesson 3 - Profit and cash are different

"A profitable business can still run out of cash."

### Lesson 4 - Owner contributions are not sales

"The money you put in is your investment in the business."

### Lesson 5 - Recurring customers are valuable

"You spend less time finding work when customers keep coming back."

### Lesson 6 - Time is a cost

"A job that takes twice as long may be less profitable even if the customer pays more."

### Lesson 7 - Nearby customers are more valuable

"Drive time doesn't earn revenue."

### Lesson 8 - Reviews create future customers

"Doing a great job today helps sell tomorrow's job."

### Lesson 9 - Systems create freedom

"If every job is recorded the same way, you know what is actually happening."

### Lesson 10 - Software is purchased to solve a problem

"Don't pay for software because businesses are 'supposed' to have it."
